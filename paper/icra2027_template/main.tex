\begin{abstract}
In shared spaces without communication, each robot must reach its goal from onboard observations while reacting to the other robots. A single policy must support both efficient goal progress and conservative collision avoidance, although the local interaction pattern changes over a rollout. We present \method, an online policy-routing framework for decentralized multi-robot navigation. The method reuses two pretrained actors, freezes them, and trains only a temporal router. The Interaction Actor was previously trained with privileged centralized supervision. At each routing instant, the router selects between their candidate actions using local LiDAR, motion state, observation history, and candidate-action disagreement. Its training signal combines a simulator-derived interaction-phase label with a same-state paired one-step reward comparison of the two frozen actors; neither signal is available at deployment. On the high-conflict multi-robot scenarios used in this study, a matched evaluation on the frozen Dense slice quantifies episode-level multi-agent full success, robot-level collision, and operating costs relative to the Navigation Actor. The numerical results are reported after the matched queue is complete.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Multi-robot navigation in a shared workspace is coupled even when robots do not communicate. A robot changes the future observations of the others through its speed, heading, and waiting decisions. Rollouts alternate between ordinary goal progress and short periods of yielding, slowing, or conflict resolution. A controller therefore needs to identify the local interaction phase and return to goal-directed motion when that phase ends.

Many learning-based systems use one policy for both behaviors. The policy must then represent two different preferences: progress in ordinary states and conservative motion during dense interactions. Episode-level selection is also insufficient. Different robots can be in different interaction states at the same time, and one robot can enter and leave an interaction within a single episode. A distance rule based on other robots' positions can indicate when conditional control would help, but that information is unavailable to a decentralized robot at deployment.

We ask whether two pretrained policies with complementary behavior can be routed from local observations when training uses both interaction-phase and action-outcome supervision. \method{} freezes a Navigation Actor and an Interaction Actor, then learns a temporal router for each robot. During training, simulator robot positions provide a binary interaction-phase target. Isolated same-state one-step branches provide a paired reward difference between the candidate actions. At deployment, the privileged states and reward values are removed, as are communication and global conflict information. Separate temporal encoders predict the two targets. A fixed bounded fusion rule combines them, followed by hysteresis and a minimum hold time before one actor is selected.

Our contribution is a deployment-constrained formulation for per-robot online routing from a frozen policy library. It combines privileged interaction-phase supervision with paired same-state action outcomes, while keeping both signals outside the deployment input. We evaluate this reward-aware router with both actors frozen during router training. Closed-loop success, safety, and operating costs are reported together. Validation admission and the matched evaluation were registered separately; the matched evaluation is analyzed only after its complete queue finishes.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Decentralized multi-robot navigation.} Geometric reciprocal-avoidance methods such as reciprocal velocity obstacles provide explicit collision constraints. CADRL introduced decentralized deep reinforcement learning for non-communicating agents \cite{vanDenBerg2008,chen2017cadrl}. Subsequent deep policies address decentralized collision avoidance and pedestrian-rich navigation \cite{long2018decentralized,everett2021collision}, and mapless navigation with sim-to-real transfer \cite{tai2017virtual}. Recent work considers certifiable safety, connectivity maintenance, perceptual hallucination in narrow spaces, communication-restricted coordination, distributional reinforcement learning, socially-aware policies, and goal-conditioned safe navigation \cite{Cui_2022,Li_2022,Park_2023,Bramblett_2023,Lin_2024,Wang_2024,Feng_2025}. Most of these methods learn one policy for navigation and interaction. We keep the local-observation deployment setting and instead study how to reuse two complementary policies without exposing neighborhood ground truth.

Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning commonly uses centralized training with decentralized execution, including actor--critic, counterfactual, and monotonic value-factorization methods \cite{lowe2017maddpg,foerster2018coma,rashid2018qmix,gronauer2021marl}. Our setting instead freezes the policy experts and learns only a per-robot selector after expert training.

\textbf{Temporal policy selection.} Options formalize temporally extended policies and termination conditions \cite{sutton1999options}. Option-Critic learns options and termination functions jointly, while deliberation costs penalize unnecessary switching \cite{bacon2017option,harb2018deliberation}. Navigation systems have learned context-dependent planner parameters or intervention-driven adaptations, including APPLD, APPLI, and APPLR \cite{xiao2020appld,wang2021appli,xu2021applr}. These methods adapt planner parameters rather than route between frozen learned policies. Other work switches between classical and learned planners \cite{kastner2022allinone,dey2023trust}, including a normalizing-flow-based switch \cite{matsumoto2024flow}. A decentralized hybrid APF/WF system also switches between local behaviors \cite{Kim_2025}. In contrast, our selector routes between two learned policy experts using local temporal evidence. \method{} selects a per-robot interaction phase rather than an episode-level mode. The policy library remains frozen during router training, privileged phase supervision is used only during training, and evaluation uses closed-loop team behavior rather than selector accuracy alone.

\textbf{Privileged supervision and closed-loop aggregation.} Asymmetric actor--critic methods let a critic use privileged state while the deployed actor uses sensors \cite{pinto2018asymmetric}. Learning by Cheating transfers information from a privileged teacher to a sensor policy \cite{chen2020cheating}. DAgger queries supervision on learner-visited states; SafeDAgger and EnsembleDAgger study intervention and uncertainty \cite{ross2011dagger,zhang2017safedagger,menda2019ensemble}. Safe control methods constrain updates or actions with costs, shields, stability guarantees, observation-based refinement, or trajectory optimization \cite{achiam2017cpo,alshiekh2018shielding,berkenkamp2017safe,dalal2018safe,Cui_2022,brunke2022safe}. \method{} uses the same training/deployment asymmetry but does not distill an expert action or value. Its privileged label is an interaction-state proxy. Student-rollout aggregation is treated as a training detail rather than an independently established source of improvement.

Off-policy continuous-control methods such as soft actor--critic and goal relabeling provide complementary training foundations for the frozen experts, but are not used as competing router designs here \cite{haarnoja2018sac,andrychowicz2017her}. Uncertainty estimation under dataset shift motivates keeping deployment-side switching evidence local and explicitly measurable \cite{ovadia2019trust}.

\section{Problem Formulation}
Consider $N$ robots. Each actor receives a 24-dimensional local observation
\begin{equation}
 o_t^i=[\ell_t^i,D_t^i,\theta_t^i,u_{t-1}^i]\in\mathbb{R}^{24},
\end{equation}
where $\ell_t^i\in\mathbb{R}^{20}$ is the 20-sector LiDAR encoding, $D_t^i$ and $\theta_t^i$ are goal distance and bearing error, and $u_{t-1}^i\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is the previous command. The deployed perception stack additionally reads the raw VLP-16 scan and ego odometry to construct router features. Robots do not exchange state, intent, or maps. Let $\pi_N$ and $\pi_I$ denote the Navigation Actor and Interaction Actor. Both receive the same deployable observation and produce candidate actions
\begin{equation}
 a_{N,t}^i=\pi_N(o_t^i),\qquad a_{I,t}^i=\pi_I(o_t^i),\qquad a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.
\end{equation}
The actors emit tanh actions. Hard selection is formed in this raw action space. Before execution, the environment maps the selected linear component to its nonnegative velocity range. For the router comparison feature, that component is mapped from $[-1,1]$ to $[0,1]$ before $\Delta a_t^i=a_{I,t}^i-a_{N,t}^i$ is formed.
The router produces a binary state $z_t^i$ and executes a hard selection,
\begin{equation}
 a_t^i=(1-z_t^i)a_{N,t}^i+z_t^i a_{I,t}^i.
 \label{eq:hardroute}
\end{equation}
The actors are not updated during router training. A robot may therefore use a different actor from another robot at the same environment time.

During training only, the simulator provides robot centers $p_t^i$. Let $\mathcal{A}_t$ contain robots that are neither at the goal nor in collision immediately before the routing decision. For the nearest active robot, the interaction target is
\begin{equation}
 d_t^i=\min_{j\neq i,\,j\in\mathcal{A}_t}\|p_t^i-p_t^j\|_2,\qquad y_t^i=\mathbb{I}[d_t^i\leq 2.0\,\mathrm{m}].
 \label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:label} is a supervision proxy. It is not a deployable distance rule and does not imply that the Interaction Actor is optimal at every positive frame. If no other active robot exists, we set $d_t^i=+\infty$ and hence $y_t^i=0$. Robots that have reached their goals or have collided are removed from $\mathcal{A}_t$ and no longer contribute routing actions. The label can therefore include nearby but non-threatening robots. This proxy noise is part of the training protocol.

\section{PIRoute}
\subsection{Frozen Policy Library and Perception}
The frozen Navigation Actor follows the TD3 actor--critic formulation~\cite{fujimoto2018td3}. We warmed its actor from the preceding curriculum checkpoint, reinitialized the critic, and trained on five-robot standard scenes with individual reward and no neighborhood critic. The checkpoint was selected by full success. It supplies default goal progress and static-obstacle handling. The frozen Interaction Actor was initialized from the Navigation Actor and trained for 320,000 agent samples under the strong-interaction curriculum. During training only, the nearest-active-robot distance selected an interaction window ($\leq 2.0$\,m). The Interaction Actor was executed and updated inside this window; the Navigation Actor handled the other states. The training critic used a privileged 87-dimensional neighborhood observation. The Interaction Actor itself retained the same 24-dimensional deployment observation and was frozen after the fixed budget. Both actors are project-internal artifacts rather than reproduced literature baselines. Training-side neighborhood information is not passed to either actor at test time.

The LiDAR front-end, G0, projects the VLP-16 scan into a local geometric representation. For each geometrically generated point-cloud candidate, it outputs a robot-shape probability in $[0,1]$ and a center-offset estimate. We retain the probability as a continuous feature instead of making a hard robot/non-robot decision. G0 was supervised with simulator robot positions during training. Its center-offset head is not used by the deployment controller, whose track centers come from geometric candidates. The preregistered hard admission criterion was precision $\geq 0.70$, recall $\geq 0.90$, and overall false-positive rate (FPR) $\leq 0.10$ (with static-obstacle FPR reported separately). G0 did not meet the complete criterion. \method{} therefore uses the continuous robot-shape probability without claiming a reliable robot detector. A deterministic tracking stage, G1, exponentially smooths this probability and adds candidate age, relative velocity, closing speed, closest-point-of-approach, and time-to-collision features. G0 and G1 are frozen before router training.

\subsection{Router Representation}
At each routing instant, the router forms a 76-dimensional local representation $q_t^i$ from local state, LiDAR candidate features, and short-term tracking. It also receives both candidate actions and their disagreement:
\begin{equation}
 \Delta a_t^i=a_{I,t}^i-a_{N,t}^i,\qquad x_t^i=[q_t^i,a_{N,t}^i,a_{I,t}^i,\Delta a_t^i]\in\mathbb{R}^{82}.
 \label{eq:routerinput}
\end{equation}
The action difference is a task-relevant cue rather than a value-function difference. It indicates when the two frozen roles recommend materially different controls.

\subsection{Temporal Estimation and Stable Switching}
The router encodes the latest $H=8$ routing frames with a GRU,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \tilde h_{p,t}^i&=\mathrm{GRU}_{p}(x_{t-H+1:t}^i,\mathbf{0})_{\mathrm{last}},\\
 \tilde h_{r,t}^i&=\mathrm{GRU}_{r}(x_{t-H+1:t}^i,\mathbf{0})_{\mathrm{last}},\\
 \ell_t^i&=f_p(\tilde h_{p,t}^i),\quad p_t^i=\sigma(\ell_t^i),\\
 A_t^i&=f_r(\tilde h_{r,t}^i),\quad A_t^i\in[-1,1],\\
 g_t^i&=\sigma\!\left(\ell_t^i+\beta A_t^i\right).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:gru}
\end{equation}
Here $p_t^i$ estimates the interaction-control phase, $A_t^i$ predicts a bounded transform of the paired one-step reward difference, and $g_t^i$ is the joint reward-aware routing score. The two GRUs have independent parameters, so sparse reward anchors do not alter the phase representation learned from the full Gate dataset. We fix $\beta=0.25$ before closed-loop evaluation. The phase branch uses weighted binary cross-entropy against Eq.~\eqref{eq:label}. The reward branch uses a scene-weighted Huber loss on audited paired branches. The router is evaluated every two environment steps. To prevent one-frame reversals, the binary state uses the combined score with an entry threshold $\tau_{\rm on}=0.43$, an exit threshold $\tau_{\rm off}=0.33$, and a minimum hold of three routing evaluations:
\begin{equation}
 z_t^i=\begin{cases}
 1,&z_{t-1}^i=0\ \land\ g_t^i\geq\tau_{\rm on},\\
 0,&z_{t-1}^i=1\ \land\ g_t^i\leq\tau_{\rm off}\ \land\ c_t^i\geq 3,\\
 z_{t-1}^i,&\text{otherwise.}
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:hysteresis}
\end{equation}
Here $z_t^i$ is initialized to zero. The counter $c_t^i$ counts router evaluations since entry into the Interaction Actor; it is reset on a mode change and incremented only while $z_t^i=1$. The selected actor recomputes an action from the new local state at every environment step. The mode is updated only at the routing stride. Only the selected action is applied to the simulator, although both actor forward passes are computed. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} shows the information boundary.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
G0 is first trained with simulator-generated candidate supervision. We then freeze G0, G1, $\pi_N$, and $\pi_I$. Navigation and student-rollout states are used to construct Eq.~\eqref{eq:routerinput} and the privileged interaction labels in Eq.~\eqref{eq:label}. The phase branch is trained against the simulator-derived interaction label. The paired reward branch described below uses only audited counterfactual anchors.

\subsection{Paired One-Step Reward Supervision}
For a stratified set of aligned anchors, two isolated one-step simulator branches execute $a_{N,t}^i$ and $a_{I,t}^i$ from the same state. Both branches use the same task reward. The fixed common reward uses $+100/-100$ for goal/collision transitions and weights $20$, $0.5$, $0.2$, $0.5$, and $0.03$ for progress, forward action, turning, obstacle, and stagnation terms. Cooperative and other auxiliary shaping terms are disabled. We define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \Delta r_t^i&=R(s_t^i,a_{I,t}^i)-R(s_t^i,a_{N,t}^i),\\
 A_t^i&=\tanh\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{clip}(\Delta r_t^i,-s,s)}{s}\right),
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:rewardtarget}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the training-only 95th percentile of the absolute per-anchor median reward difference. Under this sign convention, $\Delta r_t^i>0$ means that the Interaction Actor obtains the higher one-step reward, whereas $\Delta r_t^i<0$ favors the Navigation Actor. Terminal differences are retained as valid evidence and clipped by the bounded transform. The reward head predicts $A_t^i$. The phase and reward losses are optimized in separate branches with scene weighting for repeated counterfactual measurements. Simulator positions, branch rewards, and $\Delta r_t^i$ are training-only and never enter the deployed router. The reward-aware router is the only newly trained component; both actors and G0/G1 remain frozen.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
Both frozen actors use the same 24-dimensional observation and a $24\!\to\!800\!\to\!600\!\to\!2$ MLP with ReLU hidden layers and tanh actions. G0 is a three-layer CNN (16, 32, and 64 channels, $3\times3$ kernels, GroupNorm, ReLU, and $2\times2$ max-pooling in the first two blocks) followed by adaptive pooling and separate robot-shape and center-offset heads. Only its continuous robot-shape probability is passed to tracking. G1 associates candidates within $0.65$ m, exponentially smooths the shape probability, retains up to two missed frames, and estimates motion from a five-frame history. Its four highest-priority tracks contribute $4\times11$ features, including raw and smoothed shape probabilities. Eight global summary features complete the 76-dimensional $q_t^i$ together with the 24-dimensional actor state. Tracks are sorted by detector confidence and motion risk, then zero-padded when fewer than four are present. The 82-dimensional router input is normalized using training-split statistics.

The router uses two independent single-layer 64-hidden-unit GRUs. Each is followed by a $64\!\to\!32\!\to\!1$ head; the reward head has a final tanh to enforce the bounded target. We optimize with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) using a fixed 40-epoch phase budget and 80-epoch reward budget. Best checkpoints are selected on disjoint validation data before any closed-loop test. Counterfactual reward supervision contains 548 training records from 356 unique anchors and 336 unique scenes, and 132 validation records from 65 unique scenes. Repeated measurements are scene-weighted. Router states are reset per episode and per robot. Histories shorter than eight routing frames are left-padded with zeros, and terminated robots are excluded from subsequent routing decisions. The router mode is evaluated every two environment steps. The selected actor recomputes its action every environment step; with a 0.2-s environment step, mode updates occur at 2.5 Hz. Thresholds are $0.43/0.33$, with fusion weight $\beta=0.25$ and a minimum hold of three routing evaluations.

Experiments use ROS Noetic with Gazebo 11.15.1. One action step in the multi-robot environment advances 0.2 s. Sealed runs use fixed Gazebo physics increments of 0.001 s. A robot is successful when its center is within 0.30 m of its goal without a collision. A collision is recorded when the minimum LiDAR return is below 0.35 m. Episodes are truncated after 300 environment steps (60 s nominal simulation time). The sealed seeds and frozen checkpoint identities are fixed in the protocol and accompanying artifact records.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\subsection{Research Questions and Comparisons}
The confirmatory question is whether the reward-aware routing of the frozen policy pair improves closed-loop full success over the frozen Navigation Actor under dense multi-robot interaction. Collision is a prespecified secondary safety metric. Completion steps and Interaction Actor selection share are reported as operating costs. The earlier proximity-only router, always-on, capacity, and privileged-switch comparisons remain predecessor or supporting evidence; the earlier router is not the adoption baseline for the current method.

\subsection{Matched Evaluation}
The matched evaluation reuses the 256 fixed Dense scenes in the G25 sealed manifest (the original test slice $[0:256]$) and its three environment repeats. It compares the frozen Navigation Actor with the reward-aware router; G34 contributes 768 new episodes, while the G25 5A results are reused without rerunning that baseline. This is a matched reuse of an earlier sealed slice, not a new independent test. Scene IDs, order, physics, checkpoints, termination rules, and statistical procedures were fixed before the G34 queue was launched. Earlier router versions and boundary checks remain predecessor or supporting evidence and are not relabeled as results of the current method.

The primary metric is \emph{full success}: all five robots reach their goals within the termination condition without collision. We additionally report robot-level agent success and collision, episode timeout, raw termination steps, paired-success steps, Interaction Actor selection share, and switch counts. Full success is analyzed with scene-cluster paired bootstrap confidence intervals and a two-sided scene-level sign-flip test; repeated runs of the same scene are not treated as independent scenes. The privileged 2-m rule is diagnostic and non-deployable and is excluded from the matched evaluation.

\subsection{Implementation and Cost}
The actors and G0/G1 are frozen during router training. The router operates at a 5-Hz control loop with a two-step mode-update stride. A CPU-only, precomputed-candidate representative inference microbenchmark on an Intel Core i9-14900KF (one CPU thread, batch size 1, 200 warm-up and 2,000 timed iterations; not a training-hardware measurement) gives 1.443 ms (p95 1.490 ms) for perception/tracking features, two actor forwards, and router inference; it excludes raw point-cloud candidate extraction, ROS transport, sensor waiting, and command publication. The corresponding single Navigation Actor forward is 0.037 ms. Thus the method has computation and interaction-time costs even though it does not perform additional actor updates.

\subsection{Scenarios and Splits}
All experiments use the Gazebo multi-robot navigation simulator with five differential-drive robots unless stated otherwise. The complete mixed-scene development set contains 120 fixed scenes, 60 standard and 60 dense, evaluated with two environment repeats. The dense high-conflict development set contains 256 fixed scenes; 8, 61, 80, and 107 scenes have 0, 1, 2, and at least 3 reference-path conflict edges, respectively. Conflict edges are used only for offline stratification and never enter a policy or router input. The matched evaluation reuses the G25 sealed slice $[0:256]$ of the original dense-test manifest for a same-slice comparison with the archived 5A results. Training and validation scenes remain separate from this test slice. The post-sealed trajectory capture is a separate outcome-stratified qualitative sample and is not used to recompute any rate.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
An episode is a full success only when every robot reaches its goal within the termination condition without collision. Robot-level agent success and collision are reported separately because a team can fail even when several robots succeed; unresolved denotes a robot that is neither successful nor colliding when the episode terminates by timeout. Collision is a binary robot-level rate over all robot outcomes, not an episode-level collision rate. We distinguish raw termination steps, which are affected by early collision termination, from paired-success steps, which are computed only on matched scene/repeat episodes where both methods achieve full success. Repeated runs of a scene are clustered by scene. We use a scene-cluster BCa paired bootstrap with 20,000 resamples and a two-sided scene-level Monte Carlo sign-flip test with 100,000 draws. The preregistered decision threshold is two-sided $\alpha=0.05$; a confirmatory success claim requires a positive confidence-interval lower bound and a sign-flip $p$-value below this threshold. Percentages are pooled descriptive rates, whereas differences and intervals use the paired clustered analysis.

\subsection{Exploratory Boundary Checks}
The zero-update robot-count check gives a positive but uncertain full-success difference of 3.13 percentage points for three robots and 1.56 percentage points for seven robots; both exploratory confidence intervals cross zero. At seven robots, however, robot-level success increases by 9.54 percentage points (exploratory BCa 95\% CI [6.14, 12.72]) and robot-level collision decreases by 9.65 percentage points (95\% CI [$-12.83,-6.25$]), with a corresponding raw-step increase of 17.42. A separate normalizing-flow-inspired external switching baseline, motivated by Matsumoto et al.~\cite{matsumoto2024flow}, obtains 30.08\% full success on an independent five-robot slice, compared with 28.52\% for the Navigation Actor and 35.94\% for the proximity-only predecessor; its difference from the Navigation Actor is only 1.56 percentage points (exploratory BCa 95\% CI [$-4.30,+7.42$]). These checks are reported as scope boundaries and are not pooled with the current matched evaluation.

\section{Limitations and Discussion}
This is a system-level result under a frozen-policy reuse protocol. The final claim is restricted to the preregistered matched comparison with the Navigation Actor on the G25 slice; the earlier proximity-only router is a predecessor, not the adoption baseline. The interaction label is a distance-based proxy and can mark nearby robots that do not require avoidance. The router may consequently select the Interaction Actor often and incur a measurable successful-run step cost. The two actors also require two forward passes at each routing instant.

The capacity control uses a parameter-matched actor trained with the same Navigation Actor recipe. Its retained checkpoint was selected before widened-actor updates became effective; later unfreezing collapsed. This control does not establish that every sufficiently trained larger single actor is inferior. The cross-protocol higher-capacity result is higher on the dense development set, but its extra curriculum and budget make it unsuitable for a fair ranking. Similarly, student-rollout aggregation is part of the predecessor router's training procedure, but its comparison with the single-frame router is not significant on the validation protocol; it should not be advertised as a standalone gain.

The three- and seven-robot zero-update tests and the normalizing-flow-inspired external router are exploratory boundary checks for the predecessor system. They are not pooled with the current matched evaluation. All current results are simulated. Transfer to real LiDAR, different maps, arbitrary team sizes, and alternative policy libraries remains open.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented \method{}, a deployment-constrained reward-aware router between two frozen navigation policies. The matched G25-slice evaluation is designed to quantify full-team success, robot-level collision, and operating costs against the frozen Navigation Actor under identical scenes and repeats. The scope remains limited to the fixed simulated policy library and deployment protocol.
